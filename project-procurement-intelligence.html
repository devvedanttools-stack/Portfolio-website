Executive Summary

Always-current procurement data, without APIs

The client's procurement team depended on dozens of supplier portals that offered no API access, forcing manual copy-paste audits that were slow, inconsistent, and prone to missing critical expiry changes. I led the architecture, build, and governance program for a procurement intelligence pipeline that replaced that manual process with resilient, scheduled automation — giving the business always-current, auditable supplier data for the first time.

The Business Problem

The client's inventory and quality teams depended on product and expiry data locked inside dozens of supplier web portals with no API access. The only way to keep that data current was a manual copy-paste audit process, run inconsistently across suppliers and prone to human error. The business risk was not just the effort involved — it was missed expiry changes that led to inventory write-offs and quality incidents, and an audit trail too weak to satisfy internal controls.

A baseline audit at engagement kickoff quantified the exposure: procurement staff spent an estimated 22 hours per week on manual portal checks across the supplier base, missed or late-caught expiry changes drove an estimated $180K in annual inventory write-offs, and there was no consistent, timestamped record of when supplier data had last been verified. Leadership needed a way to keep supplier data current at scale without depending on APIs the suppliers would never build.

The Solution

I designed and delivered a governed procurement intelligence pipeline that navigates supplier portals through resilient browser automation, extracts product and expiry data through a combination of parsing and OCR, and normalizes everything into a consistent schema. Scheduled runs keep the data current automatically, and alerts fire the moment an item nears expiry or a supplier record changes — routed directly into the inventory and quality workflows the team already uses.

Every extraction run is logged with a timestamp and a reliability check that flags anomalies — a portal layout change, a failed extraction, an unexpected data gap — rather than silently propagating bad data downstream. That reliability discipline, not just the automation itself, was what made procurement and quality leadership trust the pipeline enough to retire the manual audit process entirely.
