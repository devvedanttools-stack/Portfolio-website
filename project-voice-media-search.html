Executive Summary

From "where is that file?" to instant retrieval

The client's marketing, sales, and enablement teams depended on a small number of people who happened to know where things lived in a sprawling, inconsistently named media library. I led the architecture, build, and governance program for a voice-first media intelligence platform that turned that tribal knowledge into a governed, searchable capability — reducing retrieval time from minutes to seconds and freeing the business from single-person dependency.

The Business Problem

The client's critical marketing and sales assets lived in deep, inconsistently named folder trees across shared drives. Finding the right image, video, or document depended almost entirely on knowing who to ask — usually one or two long-tenured team members — rather than any searchable system. This created adoption drag across marketing, sales, and enablement, and a real risk of lost productivity whenever those key people were unavailable.

A baseline audit at engagement kickoff quantified the exposure: average asset retrieval time of 6.2 minutes when successful, and a meaningful share of requests escalated directly to two "librarian" team members who together fielded an estimated 40 escalations per week. Leadership had no visibility into how often assets were being recreated from scratch simply because an existing one couldn't be found — a direct, if invisible, cost to marketing efficiency.

The Solution

I designed and delivered a media intelligence platform with governed indexing, voice-first queries, and ranked retrieval. A scheduled crawler keeps the index current across every folder, a speech recognition layer tuned for the client's domain vocabulary understands natural phrasing, and a ranking engine combining fuzzy matching and similarity scoring returns previewable results in seconds rather than minutes.

The platform was built to keep the index verifiably current, not just fast: every crawl run is logged, and low-confidence matches are surfaced distinctly from high-confidence ones so users can trust what they're seeing. That reliability signal — knowing whether a result is a strong match or a guess — was what got skeptical long-tenured staff to actually adopt the tool instead of falling back on asking a colleague.
